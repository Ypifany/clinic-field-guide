A PUBLIC FIELD GUIDE FOR CLINIC WORK

Cybersecurity AI Risk and Governance Field Guide

By Cheryl Abram (with help from ChatGPT to summarize sources, develop examples, create diagrams, organize and format the text, and build this website).
Ypifany · First edition, revised · October 2026

Help people understand what is happening in their digital space, make real choices, and recover when something goes wrong.

What is a cybersecurity clinic?

A cybersecurity clinic brings people learning or practicing cybersecurity together with an organization that wants help. They examine how its digital systems are used, consider risks and opportunities, and agree on practical next steps. Organizations get support, and participants build skills through the work.

This guide is built on selected practices from Cheryl Abram's original Decision Visibility Model (DVM), evaluation and re-valuation methods, and work on human–synthetic collaboration, safety, and governance and risk operations. Inspired by Cori Faklaris's CyberAI Field Guide and informed by cybersecurity clinic resources and AI risk research, it helps people examine digital systems, including cloud services and AI, so those affected can sense what is happening, make real choices, and recover.

You can begin on your own, with a partner, or with a mentor. Start with the food bank introduced in Tab 1, your own digital space, or a practice environment. For real client work, first agree with the organization on purpose, scope, methods, information handling, and who can stop the work. Follow any supervision requirements of your own clinic or institution.

Every tab practices part of a client engagement. Tab 9 brings the findings and next steps together. Use the checks in each tab and the self-assessment in Tab 10 to review your work; a mentor is optional.

Choose tools that fit the people and task. Capture your work in drawings, notes, photographs, slides, or the clinic's existing systems. The methods here are options; use an equivalent that works for you.

The guide's definitions and working rules

Cybersecurity gives you the ability to sense what is happening in your digital space so you can make choices. Sensing includes discovering exposure before an event, noticing events as they happen, and understanding consequences afterward. Ask whose visibility is missing.

Safety means the person affected can sense what is happening, make a real choice, and recover if something goes wrong. All three must be present. A feature working as designed can still harm a person.

Risk is the chance that something changes, plus what that change would mean for you. Helpful and harmful changes both count. Name who gains and who carries each consequence.

Vulnerability in this guide means a place where change is easy. Whether the change matters is a separate risk question. Conventional technical usage also matters: NIST definitions commonly describe an exploitable or triggerable weakness. Keep the meaning being used clear.

Governance is how choices get made, who makes them, and how you check later whether they worked. Delegating a decision does not erase the choice to delegate.

A change agent can be a person, a storm, an attacker, or another cause of change. A threat is a change someone would not want; an opportunity is a change they would want. Always name whose perspective is being used.

AI use and human judgment

Use the AI and information approved for the engagement. AI may suggest, organize, draft, and help test. Check its contributions against evidence and the people involved. Explain what you accept, revise, or reject.

An AI agent uses AI to choose steps and use tools toward a goal. In this guide, reflexive responses are decided ahead of time and run within an agreed scope; reading, recommending, and small reversible actions may fit. Deliberate responses require a person to decide each time. Sending, deleting, spending, and granting access are deliberate.

Technical permission, permission for a purpose, and affected-person consent are separate. Renew authorization when the goal changes or the agent reaches new people or systems. If outside instructions try to redirect the agent, stop and alert; if behavior drifts from the task, pause and show the difference.

Limits before action

Agree on scope, methods, information handling, and who can stop. Separate a harmful outcome from an earlier warning that triggers review. State the response and what must be demonstrated before resuming. A reviewer needs a preview, enough understanding to question it, and a working way to stop.

Cloud systems

Find out which controls the client operates, which the provider supplies, and what evidence the clinic can obtain. Sharing permissions in a ready-made application and security settings in cloud infrastructure require different methods and skills. Use the provider's current shared responsibility guidance for the service being examined.

TAB 01

Understand the Situation

What situation are people trying to understand, create, preserve, or change?

Begin with the situation and the current explanation of it. Identify the client, affected people, expected benefit, assumptions, and what the engagement can examine. Keep facts, interpretations, and unknowns distinguishable.

Meet the practice client

A fictional food bank runs through all ten tabs. Staff and volunteers use cloud email and shared folders to answer donor questions. The director is considering an AI assistant to speed up replies.

Treat each example as the next part of this engagement. Use it to practice what you would ask, draw, verify, and recommend. If a detail is missing, mark it unknown; ask AI to suggest a question rather than invent the answer. Later tabs add discoveries.

See it at the food bank

The director sees slow donor replies; a volunteer describes searching three cloud folders for the right answer. You draw both accounts and keep the cause of the delay open until there is evidence.

TAB 02

Discover How Work Happens

TAB 03

See the System and Its Dependencies

What connects to what, where does information go, and what are we agreeing to examine?

Draw a simple network and system map: devices, Wi-Fi or a router, cloud services, accounts, and any AI service. Trace the earlier decisions that allow each connection. Separate the account owner, the person affected, and the people trusted with access. Include dependencies beyond the organization buying the system.

Read and make a simple map

A network map shows how devices connect. A system map also shows the services, accounts, people, and information they depend on. Combine them in one sketch so everyone can see what you are examining.

  • Boxes: name a device, router, cloud service, or account.
  • Arrows: label what connects or what moves, and in which direction.
  • A dashed boundary: mark the agreed clinic scope. Show outside dependencies too.
  • Question marks: mark connections or permissions you still need to confirm.

Draft it early. Check it against the inventory in Tab 4, use it to locate changes in Tab 5 and evidence in Tab 7, and show it with findings in Tab 9. You do not need IP addresses or secrets for this introductory map.

See it at the food bank

For the practice sketch, assume a staff laptop reaches cloud email, shared folders, and a separate AI service through Wi-Fi and the internet. Staff manually paste approved sample text into AI and review its draft. A direct folder-to-AI connection has not been established. You mark the provider-access and retention questions for checking.

What to say

A practice conversation: listen for details to explore, then check them.
Participant saysAvoid leading withAsk insteadListen for

Try it

Sketch this practice system, your own digital space, or an agreed part of a client system. Label the connections, mark the boundary, and list what you have not confirmed. Keep a simple system map and a record of dependencies and responsibilities in notes, a drawing, or another format that suits the work.

Ways to explore it

    Connect objects for devices and services. Remove one connection and discuss what stops working.

AI can help

Review and decide

Check every box, arrow, and boundary against your notes and, for a client engagement, with the people who use or manage the system. Keep assumptions visible. Verify current provider information before claiming what it receives or retains.

Keep in view
For each connection, ask what crosses it and who chose to allow that.

TAB 04

Inventory What Is Here

What capabilities, resources, and exposures are actually available?

Inventory devices, accounts, data, software, AI models, memory, tools, and allowed actions. Record access, logging, updates, and recovery arrangements. Include people's time, knowledge, relationships, and ability to act. Mark what is controlled directly, shared, or outside the engagement's control. Check the Tab 3 diagram against the inventory and correct missing or unverified connections.

See it at the food bank

The team compares the food bank's account list with approved cloud sharing screens. It finds an old volunteer account still has access to a folder. Available records show the access grant; they do not yet establish whether the account was used.

TAB 05

Examine Risk in Context

Where is change easy, and what could it mean for different people?

Model useful opportunities and unwanted changes, including harmful use by a technically authorized person. Examine relationships, technical boundaries, and changing circumstances. Use conventional threat methods alongside the guide's broader definitions, with any extensions clearly labeled.

See it at the food bank

Easier folder sharing could help volunteers answer donors sooner. It could also expose information to someone whose access is no longer appropriate. The team names both changes and asks who gains, who carries the consequence, and whose view is missing.

What to say

A practice conversation: listen for details to explore, then check them.
Participant saysAvoid leading withAsk insteadListen for
Sharing the folder with everyone would make our work easier.So open sharing is unsafe?Who would benefit from that change, who else could be affected, and how could they see or question it?Useful opportunities and unwanted changes, whose perspective is missing, and whether affected people have a real choice.

Try it

Describe one change as seen by the owner and by the person carrying its consequences. Use the system map to locate where that change could travel. Keep a scenario record covering change agents, vulnerabilities, consequences, and missing senses in notes, a drawing, or another format that suits the work.

Ways to explore it

  • OWASP Cornucopia
    Use the online application-security cards to prompt questions the team might overlook.
  • OWASP Threat Dragon
    Explore a sample model, then map an authorized system and possible threats.
  • What changes discussion
    Introduce a changed role, failed service, or new goal and discuss what it means to each person.

AI can help

Generate possible helpful and harmful changes from the approved situation. Use the suggestions to broaden inquiry.

Review and decide

Treat scenarios as possibilities until evidence supports them. Check feasibility, affected people, and useful opportunities. Do not let a threat score replace their perspective.

Keep in view
Ease of change is one question. What it means to someone is another.

TAB 06

Design Human and AI Collaboration

What work can be delegated while people retain meaningful judgment?

Match the person, task, AI capability, interaction, and operating context. Record the goal, tool limits, evidence requirements, reflexive and deliberate actions, and renewal conditions. Demonstrate the ability to review and stop.

See it at the food bank

The food bank permits an assistant to draft replies from approved sample material. A person reviews each recipient and message before sending. A rehearsal reveals that the reviewer cannot see the attachment, so the team keeps that action outside the assistant's scope until oversight works.

TAB 07

Test Claims and Controls

What evidence answers the actual question?

Set context and anticipate errors before AI use. Review scope, depth, currency, system fit, source, completeness, and requirement match. Investigate doubts the checklist misses. Examine evidence, interview people, or test controls with permission. Keep a shared error log.

See it at the food bank

AI says access reviews happen every quarter because a policy requires them. The team separates that policy from evidence of completed reviews. A dated review record supports the work it documents; missing records leave questions to investigate.

TAB 08

Put Governance into Practice

Who can choose, refuse, intervene, and revisit the decision?

Record decision authority, delegation, purpose, consent, and who carries consequences. Connect access changes to changing purposes and relationships. Specify signals, review dates, stop authority, notification, and recovery support. Include a route for the affected person when the account owner is the source of harm.

The former volunteer's access was granted for a role that has ended. The team asks who can review the grant, who could be affected by leaving it active, and how people can raise a concern. It drafts a rule with a review trigger and an actual person able to intervene.

TAB 09

Present Findings and Agree on Next Steps

Can the client and affected people understand the findings and make a real choice about what follows?

Use the work developed from Tab 1 onward, including the system map. Present supported findings, uncertainty, options, and consequences. Review the recommendations against the evidence and invite the client to correct the account. Seek additional expertise for decisions you cannot adequately assess. Agree on responsibilities, recovery support, follow-up, and closure of temporary access.

See it at the food bank

The team presents two options for folder access and AI drafting. The food bank chooses the smaller change because staff can maintain it now. The team names what that choice leaves unresolved and agrees who will review it after the next volunteer turnover.

TAB 10

Evaluate and Revalue

What can people see now, and what should happen next?

Review the evidence and feedback from affected people to assess whether they can better sense what is happening, make real choices, and recover when something goes wrong. Identify improvements, useful opportunities, and unresolved gaps, then explain what needs follow-up and who will take responsibility.

See it at the food bank

At follow-up, staff can see sharing changes sooner and stop an AI draft before sending. They also report that account recovery instructions are still hard to use. You record the visibility and choice gains, keep the recovery gap open, and revisit the earlier decision.

Use the observations and feedback to recommend what to continue, change, stop, or check next. Explain the evidence and name who will take responsibility. Keep a short outcome review with evidence, unresolved gaps, and agreed follow-up in notes, a drawing, or another format that suits the work.

AI can help

Compare approved before-and-after observations, identify differences from expectations, and suggest follow-up questions.

Review and decide

Check the comparison with the client and affected people. Separate observation from explanation. Decide whether to continue, stop, change, or observe, using preserved choice and meaningful recovery as priorities.

Keep in view
Show what people can now sense, choose, and recover, and what still needs attention.

Check your work

  • Can I compare the original expectation with a before-and-after observation?
  • Have I asked affected people what changed for their visibility, choice, and recovery?
  • Can I explain whether to continue, change, stop, or observe, using evidence and remaining gaps?

Use: I can show this / I need to check this / I need help. Point to evidence or name your next step.

Review your own work

Choose I can show this, I need to check this, or I need help for each question. Point to evidence or name a next step. A mentor can help, but you can use these checks on your own. Practice missing skills with the fictional case; narrow or pause any real client work that exceeds your agreement or abilities.

  1. Can I show the agreed scope and explain how I stayed within it?
  2. Can I explain the diagram's boundary and identify anything I could not verify?
  3. Can I trace my main findings to evidence and state what remains unknown?
  4. Can I show how affected people's perspectives influenced the work?
  5. Can I identify an AI contribution I accepted, changed, or rejected and explain why?
  6. Can I show how my recommendations support sensing, real choice, and meaningful recovery, or name the remaining gap?
  7. Can I identify a limit in my own skills and decide what to continue, check, narrow, pause, or seek help with?

If you are building a portfolio, return to the professional-work crosswalk. Keep an example of your work and explain the context, your contribution, AI assistance, evidence checks, and limits. Label it as fictional practice, personal-system practice, sandbox work, or authorized client work.

This is a review of your work, not a score or certification. For an unfamiliar situation, start again with what you can show, what remains unknown, and the limits of your agreement.
